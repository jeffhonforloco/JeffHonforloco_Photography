What separates a good portrait from a stunning one? It's rarely the camera. After years shooting portraits in Providence and across New England, I've learned that the secrets are in the details most photographers overlook.

These aren't technical tricks — they're the working methods that elevate portraits from competent to captivating.

1. The Eyes Have to Be Alive

The single biggest factor in a compelling portrait is the eyes. Not sharpness — life. Dead eyes kill an image no matter how perfect the lighting is.

I keep models engaged between frames with conversation, direction, and energy. The moment someone "checks out" mentally, it shows in their eyes. I'd rather have a slightly soft image with alive eyes than a technically perfect image with empty ones.

Catchlights matter too. That small reflection of the light source in the eyes is what makes them look luminous. No catchlight, no life. I always check for it.

2. Shoot Through the Awkward Phase

The first 15 minutes of any portrait session are usually stiff. The subject is nervous, the poses are forced, nothing quite works. Most photographers panic. I expect it.

I use this time for test shots — checking light, trying angles, letting the subject warm up. By minute 20, they're relaxed. By minute 45, we're getting the best work. The secret is not rushing the warm-up.

3. Light the Face, Not the Scene

Amateur portrait photographers light the room. Professionals light the face. Every lighting decision should start with: how does this fall on the subject's features?

I position my key light based on face shape. Rounder faces get more side light for sculpting. Angular faces get softer, more frontal light. There's no universal setup — the light serves the individual.

4. The Chin Controls Everything

Chin position is the most powerful posing variable. Chin slightly down and forward (the "turtle") defines the jawline and eliminates double chins. Chin up opens the face but can look arrogant. Chin to the side adds dynamism.

I adjust chin position for almost every frame. It's a millimeter game — tiny movements create dramatically different results. This is why directed portraits look better than selfies.

5. Negative Space Is a Compositional Tool

Don't fill every inch of the frame. Negative space — empty areas around the subject — creates breathing room, emphasizes the subject, and gives images a gallery quality. It's especially powerful for editorial and fine-art portraits.

6. Post-Production Starts Before the Shoot

I shoot with the final edit in mind. If I know I'm going for a moody black-and-white, I'll light for contrast. If the deliverable is bright and airy, I'll expose accordingly. Fixing it in post is always worse than getting it right in camera.

This also means communicating with the retoucher (or my future self) about intent. The shoot and the edit are one process, not two separate steps.

7. Connection Beats Perfection

The portraits people treasure aren't the most technically perfect — they're the ones where something real comes through. My job is to create the conditions for that: comfort, trust, and enough time for guards to drop.

Technical skill gets you in the door. Emotional intelligence is what creates stunning portraits.
